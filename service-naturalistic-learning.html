Naturalistic Learning & NET

Purposeful teaching through play, interests, and everyday routines.

Naturalistic learning and NET: what’s the difference?

These ideas overlap. On this website, naturalistic learning means learning through meaningful play, interactions, and everyday routines. It can happen spontaneously or with planned support; the phrase does not describe one specific teaching procedure.

Natural Environment Teaching (NET) is a planned teaching approach often used in ABA within those everyday settings. The clinician identifies a useful skill, follows the child’s interests, adjusts support, and tracks progress. “Natural environment” can mean a living room, mealtime, or play—not just being outdoors.

How teaching takes place

  1. Choose a meaningful goal. The clinical team and family identify a useful skill and the child’s current abilities.
  2. Find a natural opportunity. Play, a shared book, or a daily routine provides a setting for practice.
  3. Offer the right support. The clinician may model a response or provide a planned cue, then reduce help as the child learns.
  4. Connect the response to what happens next. For example, a request for a turn leads to a turn in the activity. This is a natural consequence; when it strengthens the skill, it functions as reinforcement.
  5. Review and adapt. The team records progress and checks whether the skill is useful with other people and in other routines.

The difference in everyday play

Naturalistic learning: A child explores a pretend town with a parent, discovering new words and ways to play together.

NET: During that same play, a clinician intentionally supports an agreed communication goal—for example, asking for a turn using the child’s communication method. A request leads to a turn, and the clinician records how much help was needed. Support follows the child’s needs and willingness to participate.

How Beyond can support your family

Within an individualized in-home plan, the team can connect goals to activities your child enjoys and help caregivers recognize practical opportunities for learning. Basic needs and access to communication are not things a child should have to earn.
