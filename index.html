Our Story

Beyond began long before it had a name.

When my oldest daughter was four, her pre-K teacher encouraged me to seek an autism evaluation. A diagnosis followed. I remember feeling lost and scared, hearing what she might never do when I needed help seeing what was possible.

Our dining room became a learning space. Through play, daily routines, and patience, I supported her learning and celebrated each step forward. Years later, my work in ABA deepened what I had first learned as a mother.

I created Beyond Adventures & Growth™ because I have never forgotten that uncertainty. I want families to feel heard, their children to feel valued, and no parent to feel they must find their way alone.

Naturalistic Learning & NET

Purposeful teaching through play, interests, and everyday routines.

Learn MoreShow Less +

Naturalistic learning and NET: what’s the difference?

These ideas overlap. On this website, naturalistic learning means learning through meaningful play, interactions, and everyday routines. It can happen spontaneously or with planned support; the phrase does not describe one specific teaching procedure.

Natural Environment Teaching (NET) is a planned teaching approach often used in ABA within those everyday settings. The clinician identifies a useful skill, follows the child’s interests, adjusts support, and tracks progress. “Natural environment” can mean a living room, mealtime, or play—not just being outdoors.

How teaching takes place

  1. Choose a meaningful goal. The clinical team and family identify a useful skill and the child’s current abilities.
  2. Find a natural opportunity. Play, a shared book, or a daily routine provides a setting for practice.
  3. Offer the right support. The clinician may model a response or provide a planned cue, then reduce help as the child learns.
  4. Connect the response to what happens next. For example, a request for a turn leads to a turn in the activity. This is a natural consequence; when it strengthens the skill, it functions as reinforcement.
  5. Review and adapt. The team records progress and checks whether the skill is useful with other people and in other routines.

The difference in everyday play

Naturalistic learning: A child explores a pretend town with a parent, discovering new words and ways to play together.

NET: During that same play, a clinician intentionally supports an agreed communication goal—for example, asking for a turn using the child’s communication method. A request leads to a turn, and the clinician records how much help was needed. Support follows the child’s needs and willingness to participate.

How Beyond can support your family

Within an individualized in-home plan, the team can connect goals to activities your child enjoys and help caregivers recognize practical opportunities for learning. Basic needs and access to communication are not things a child should have to earn.

Communication & Connection

Supporting expression, choices, self-advocacy, and shared experiences.

Learn MoreShow Less +

More than spoken words

Communication can include speech, gestures, signs, pictures, and a communication device. Augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) can supplement speech or provide another way to communicate. A speech-language pathologist can help assess communication needs and appropriate AAC options.

What support may involve

  • Learning accessible ways to ask for help, choose an activity, decline, or request a break.
  • Practicing communication during play and routines that matter to the child.
  • Supporting shared enjoyment and participation without requiring a child to communicate or socialize in only one way.
  • Helping caregivers recognize and respond to the child’s existing communication.

An everyday example

During a shared game, a child indicates “finished” using a picture or gesture. The adult acknowledges the message and supports ending or changing the activity. Being understood is part of connection, including when a child is expressing a boundary.

How Beyond can support your family

The clinical team can build functional communication goals into an ABA plan and, with permission, coordinate with the child’s speech-language pathologist. ABA does not replace speech-language evaluation or therapy. The aim is useful communication and comfortable participation—not forced eye contact or a promised increase in speech.

Learn more: ASHA: communication and AAC.

Independence & Daily Living

Building participation in the routines that matter to your family.

Learn MoreShow Less +

What independence can mean

Independence is personal. It may mean completing more of a routine, making a choice, using an accommodation, or asking for help. A child can gain independence while still benefiting from ongoing support.

How skills are taught

A clinician may break a routine into smaller steps—a process called task analysis—and identify which steps the child can already do. Teaching can include demonstrations, picture cues, practice, and carefully planned assistance. The team reviews progress and adjusts help rather than expecting every step at once.

  • Getting dressed or preparing belongings for the day.
  • Participating in handwashing and other self-care routines.
  • Helping with simple household tasks.
  • Communicating when a task is uncomfortable or help is needed.

An everyday example

For packing a bag, a picture checklist might show a book, lunch container, and water bottle. A child may already pack one item independently and need a reminder for another. The plan focuses on the next useful step while preserving needed supports.

How Beyond can support your family

In-home support can focus on routines chosen with your family, with attention to privacy, dignity, preferences, and safety. Medical, motor, feeding, or sensory concerns may require coordination with other qualified professionals. Goals and teaching methods follow assessment; these examples are not instructions for a particular child.

Family Guidance & New Jersey Resources

Support through Beyond

Family guidance within an agreed ABA plan may include listening to your priorities, explaining goals in everyday language, demonstrating a planned support, practicing together, and reviewing what feels manageable at home. Caregiver coaching is a partnership—not an expectation that you become your child’s full-time therapist.

An everyday example

If getting ready feels difficult, you can bring that concern to the clinical team. Together, you can discuss the routine, the child’s needs, and possible supports. The clinician can explain and demonstrate the agreed approach, invite questions, and review whether it helps.

Community-Based Learning

Making room for participation, choice, and belonging beyond home.

Learn MoreShow Less +

What this means

Community learning focuses on using meaningful skills and supports in places a child and family want to visit. It may involve a library, park, recreation activity, or shop. Being present is not the only goal: comfort, communication, access, and the child’s choices matter too.

What planning may include

  • Choosing an outing that matters to the child and family.
  • Considering noise, crowds, accessibility, timing, and opportunities for a break.
  • Practicing a relevant skill in a familiar setting before trying it elsewhere.
  • Planning communication, adult support, and safety arrangements.
  • Reviewing the experience and changing the plan when needed.

An everyday example

A family would like to visit the library. Preparation might include looking at pictures of the space and identifying how the child can request a break. An initial visit could be brief, with the option to leave. Participation is adjusted to the child rather than measured by how long they tolerate discomfort.

How Beyond can support your family

Beyond’s initial focus is in-home ABA. Goals may include preparation for everyday outings and discussion of supports with caregivers. Direct community sessions require separate confirmation of clinical suitability, staffing, permission, availability, and funding authorization where applicable. Transportation, supervision at events, and camp services are not automatically included.

For possible NJ summer-camp financial assistance and one-to-one aide support, eligible families can explore PerformCare’s community and camp resources. Funding and availability are limited; this is a separate program.

School Collaboration

Helping the adults around a child share useful information.

Learn MoreShow Less +

What collaboration can look like

Children may need different supports at school and at home. With appropriate consent, the clinical team can exchange relevant observations with school professionals, discuss communication and daily routines, and clarify each team’s responsibilities.

  • Sharing the child’s strengths, interests, and ways of communicating.
  • Discussing relevant progress or patterns across settings.
  • Coordinating compatible supports when appropriate, rather than assuming the same strategy fits every setting.
  • Helping families prepare questions and summarize observations for a meeting.

An everyday example

A child uses a visual routine at home, while school transitions remain difficult. With family permission and school agreement, the teams can compare observations and discuss whether a familiar visual support fits the school context. The school team makes its educational decisions through its own process.

Understanding school support in New Jersey

An Individualized Education Program (IEP) describes special education and related services for an eligible student. Parents participate in its development. A parent can make a written referral to the district for a special-education evaluation; eligibility is determined through the district’s evaluation process.

The NJDOE parent page links to PRISE, which explains special-education rights, and information about SPAN and district Special Education Parent Advisory Groups (SEPAGs). These resources can help families learn about the process and connect with support.

Beyond’s role

Any school collaboration depends on consent, school agreement, service scope, and availability. Beyond does not replace the Child Study Team, provide legal representation, decide eligibility or placement, or guarantee school-based services. Clinical and educational roles remain distinct.

Learn more: NJDOE parental rights, evaluations, and parent support.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers to the questions families may be carrying.

Does Beyond serve adults?

Beyond is being developed with a lifespan vision. Launch populations and services will depend on clinical appropriateness, licensing, staffing, funding, and current program capacity.

Is an autism diagnosis required?

Insurance-based ABA services may require an autism diagnosis and a prescription or referral depending on the payer and applicable requirements. Beyond may also develop supports that are not limited to one diagnosis, subject to scope, licensing, funding, and program availability.

How many hours will be recommended?

A responsible recommendation cannot be made before an appropriate assessment. Recommendations depend on individual needs, goals, functioning, environment, participation, medical necessity when applicable, and funding requirements.

How are staff selected and prepared?

Beyond considers experience, training, communication style, schedule, clinical needs, environmental demands, and overall fit. Staff receive orientation, plan-specific preparation, supervision, and ongoing support according to their role.

Does Beyond use table-based teaching?

Structured teaching may be used when clinically appropriate. Beyond also emphasizes naturalistic teaching because skills need to be useful during everyday routines and in real environments.

Can Beyond work with a school?

School collaboration or school-based services may be available when clinically appropriate and when required consent, authorization, funding, school approval, and operational arrangements are in place.

Are parents required to participate?

Caregiver involvement depends on the person’s age, service model, living situation, treatment goals, clinical recommendations, and funding requirements. Caregiver education is intended to build understanding and confidence — not blame or evaluate families.

Will ABA change my child’s personality?

The purpose of ethical, individualized ABA should not be to erase personality, identity, harmless differences, interests, or individuality. Goals should address meaningful skills and barriers related to communication, safety, participation, autonomy, learning, relationships, independence, and quality of life.
