New Jersey A–Z Resource Guide

Everyday needs matter, too. Explore help with services and benefits, a haircut that feels more comfortable, equipment for home, and places to enjoy time together.

This growing first edition includes 74 starting points, programs and businesses—not every provider in New Jersey. Listings are alphabetical. Statewide directories can help you find additional options near your ZIP code.

Finding a therapy or support that fits

Start with the concern you want help with and ask for an appropriate assessment. This edition includes pathways for ABA/autism services, speech-language therapy and AAC, occupational and physical therapy, feeding-related services, psychology, rehabilitation and early intervention. It also includes examples of equine programs and hospital-based music therapy.

Ask who delivers the service, what credentials they hold, how goals are chosen with your family, and how progress and your child’s comfort are considered. Recreational activities, adaptive riding and clinical therapy are different services; clarify what a program actually provides. Art therapy, aquatic therapy and other specialty-provider listings remain areas for expansion.

Planning a family fun day

Check the venue’s own calendar before choosing a date. Ask about crowds, sound and lighting, quiet spaces, bathrooms, parking, accessibility, food rules, caregiver tickets and cancellation policies. Sensory-friendly sessions may require advance registration and may run only on selected dates.

Wills, trusts and planning for the future

Families with limited income deserve access to planning help. Start with a legal-aid intake and explain exactly which documents you need. Ask whether the program offers free preparation, advice only, a referral, reduced fees or a payment plan.

Topics to discuss with a qualified NJ attorney include a will, special-needs or pooled trust, powers of attorney, healthcare directives, beneficiary designations and future caregiving arrangements. Ask how each choice could affect SSI, Medicaid and other benefits. A general trust or an ABLE account should not be assumed to replace individualized estate planning.

Questions to bring: Do you draft special-needs trusts? Who qualifies for free help? Are signing arrangements included? What setup, trustee or annual fees apply? If you cannot help, who accepts referrals?

Travel with an autistic child: food, sensory needs and airport support

Before booking

  • Choose around your child’s interests and preferences. Ask about crowd levels, sound, lighting, quiet spaces, accessible bathrooms and leaving and re-entering attractions. “Autism-friendly” does not mean every experience suits every child.
  • For a hotel, ask about a quiet room location, blackout curtains, refrigerator or kitchenette, familiar food delivery and flexible cancellation. Get important arrangements confirmed directly.
  • Review photos, maps or a short video together if your child finds previews helpful. Plan fewer activities and leave room for breaks and changes.

Food preferences and sensory-based eating needs

  • List familiar foods, brands, textures, temperatures, utensils and ways of serving food that matter to your child. Check menus and nearby grocery stores before leaving.
  • Ask venues about bringing familiar foods and any disability-related exception process. Dietary preferences, sensory needs, allergies and medically prescribed diets can require different arrangements—explain the actual need.
  • Pack accepted snacks when permitted, including a delay backup. Check storage and refrigeration needs. Look up airport rules for the specific foods; do not assume all drinks, purees, spreads or ice packs use the same rules. Check destination customs restrictions for international travel.
  • Build in regular food and drink opportunities and familiar routines. A trip does not need to become a food-expansion project. If your child already has a feeding or medical plan, arrange travel details with the treating team.

At the airport and on the plane

  • Contact TSA Cares about screening support before departure; separately contact your airline’s accessibility team about navigating the airport, boarding, connections and seating needs.
  • Tell the gate agent if your child needs extra time or assistance to board or get seated because of a disability. DOT describes a right to preboarding for passengers who identify this need. Confirm which accompanying caregiver may board with your child.
  • Ask about disability-related seating needs early; do not assume a free upgrade, a particular seat, or automatic seating for the entire party. Check the airline’s procedures.
  • Newark Terminal A has sensory spaces; check current locations and access for your itinerary. Optional Sunflower lanyards communicate a possible need for understanding and support; they do not replace assistance requests.
  • Keep communication tools, chargers, familiar comfort items and preferred hearing protection within reach, subject to screening and flight-safety rules. A short note can explain what helps: “Please speak one step at a time and allow extra processing time.”
  • If support is not working, ask for the airline’s Complaint Resolution Official (CRO). Airport or airline assistance does not replace the family’s caregiving support.

During the trip

Use a simple plan your child can understand, offer manageable choices, and keep a way to request a break available. Identify a quieter place before it is needed. Allow time to recover between outings, and change the plan if an activity is uncomfortable.

Planning suggestions, not an individualized treatment plan. Sources: National Autistic Society, US DOT, Newark Airport, and the destination links below. Check current TSA food and assistance requirements directly before travel.

Activities across ages, interests and support needs

Autistic people have different interests, strengths and support needs. This guide welcomes children, teens and adults, including people who enjoy independent outings and those who need substantial assistance. Speaking ability alone does not tell you which supports someone needs.

Choose by the person’s interests: sports, gaming, art, science, music, nature, shopping, volunteering or time with peers. An ordinary community activity with the right accommodations may be a good fit; a disability-specific program is another option. Ask the person what they enjoy and how they prefer to participate.

  • Age and choice: Are the activities age-appropriate? Are there teen or adult groups? Can participants choose activities or leave one they dislike?
  • Participation: Is this independent attendance, caregiver-accompanied recreation or a staffed support program? Are one-to-one supports available or must you arrange them?
  • Communication: Will staff support AAC, visual directions, extra processing time, gestures or other communication preferences?
  • Personal support: What help is available with mobility, toileting, eating or medical needs? Who is responsible? Ask before registration rather than assuming it is included.
  • Sensory comfort: Are quieter sessions, breaks and low-crowd areas available? Can the person keep their preferred sensory tools?
  • Safety and access: Discuss individualized supervision needs, entrances/exits, emergency procedures, staff training and any support-plan requirements directly with the program.

Listings do not mean a program can meet every support need. Confirm the fit together with the participant and provider. Search “adult,” “peer,” “art,” “sports,” “playground” or “individualized assistance” to explore options.
